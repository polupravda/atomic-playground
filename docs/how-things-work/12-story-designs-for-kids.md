## What the review found

The older designs are correct, but they were written from the science
outward. Read as a child would meet them, four things stand out:

1. **Too many new words at once.** The power plant story introduced five
   terms in a minute (control rods, moderation, delayed neutrons, k,
   SCRAM). The built stories, tested with kids, teach two to four.
2. **Several lessons per story.** The candle taught conservation of mass,
   wax chemistry, soot and why flames are yellow. Each is true; together
   they bury the surprise.
3. **Payoffs were tables and counters.** A before/after table is proof for
   a grown-up. A child needs a picture that makes them say "wait, what?"
4. **No question to carry the child through.** Only the candle and the
   tree opened with a mystery. The others began with "this is a …".

## The rules every story below follows

- **One question.** The story opens with a question a child would really
  ask, shown on the canvas in big letters and spoken.
- **One aha.** One sentence the child can repeat at dinner. Everything in
  the story serves it; background facts go to the demo's info panel.
- **Two to four new words**, the level of the built stories (which kids
  have tested), each taught the usual way (named, repeated slowly, a
  pause). Do not over-simplify: a term the picture shows is named.
- **A hero to follow**, marked with the amber ring, wherever the story is
  about atoms moving.
- **Five or six beats, about a minute**, each beat one clear picture.
- **A proof you can see**, not a number: a fogged glass, a scale that does
  not move, six arms on a mitten.
- **True and honest, including the uncomfortable parts** (the power
  plant's waste is said in the story). Nothing false is said or drawn.
- **No separate grown-ups' layer.** Each demo has the same info panel as
  every built one (How it works · Honest notes · A true story), written
  at the same level as theirs; the notes per story below go there.
- **No cards at the end.** A story ends on its last picture and one
  spoken closing line, like the built stories.

Narration follows [10-narration-strategy.md](10-narration-strategy.md)
(EN · DE · RU, science pacing, show first for data, flow for anything
that must not freeze).

## The carbon atom's adventures (a thread through four stories)

The hero of 🧒 You are star stuff is a carbon atom with an amber ring.
The candle, the tree and the diamond stories are about carbon too, so the
**same hero** appears in all of them, drawn the same way (decided
2026-11-19): the nucleus with its symbolic blue electron circles, as the
bomb demos draw their atoms, plus the amber ring. Every other atom in
these stories uses the same look, so the app keeps one visual identity
for "an atom". A child who
watches them in any order meets an old friend: born in a star, breathed
out of a candle, built into a tree, squeezed into a diamond. No story
depends on another; each only ends with one line that points onward.

## 🕯️ Where does the candle go?

- **The question:** "A candle gets shorter and shorter. Where does it go?"
- **The aha:** *The candle does not disappear. It turns into gas and
  water that float away, and you just can't see them.*
- **New words:** molecule, carbon dioxide, soot.
- **Hero:** our carbon atom, sitting in the wax.

Beats:

1. **The birthday cake.** Candles burn while a song plays (a few notes,
   no voice). They are clearly shorter at the end. The question appears.
2. **Inside the wax.** Zoom in: wax is made of carbon atoms (dark) and
   hydrogen atoms (small, white), holding hands in a chain. Our hero gets
   its amber ring.
3. **Into the flame.** Heat pulls the chain apart. Oxygen atoms from the
   air (red, in pairs) rush in. Our hero grabs two of them: "This is
   called carbon dioxide. ... Carbon. Dioxide." It floats up, and it is
   invisible, so the picture draws it faintly.
4. **The other half.** The hydrogen atoms grab oxygen too, and that makes
   water. Proof you can see: a cold glass held over the flame fogs up
   with tiny drops. "A candle makes water!"
5. **Why the flame is yellow.** Some carbon atoms do not find oxygen
   straight away. They clump into tiny specks that glow yellow-hot. "This
   is called soot. ... Soot." A spoon held in the flame turns black.
6. **The proof.** The same candle burns inside a closed jar standing on a
   scale. It burns, then goes out when the oxygen is used up. The scale's
   needle has not moved at all. Beside it, two piles of atom balls,
   before and after: the same balls, only holding different hands.
7. **Closing line.** "Our carbon atom floats away through the window.
   Maybe a leaf will catch it."

**The picture to remember:** the fogged glass, and the needle that stays
still.

**Info panel (Honest notes · A true story):** a little soot and carbon monoxide are
left over; real wax chains are 20 to 30 carbons long; the jar experiment
is Lavoisier's (1770s), the question is Faraday's 1848 Christmas lecture
for children.

**Changed from the earlier design:** the fogged glass is new (water becomes
something you see); the atom table became two piles of balls.

## 🌳 A tree is made of air

- **The question:** "A seed is tiny. A tree is heavier than a car. Where
  does all that wood come from?"
- **The aha:** *Not from the ground. A tree builds itself out of air,
  water and sunlight.*
- **New words:** carbon dioxide, photosynthesis, sugar.
- **Hero:** our carbon atom, floating in the air inside a carbon dioxide
  molecule.

Beats:

1. **The seed and the giant.** A seed on a fingertip; a time-lapse grows
   it into a tree with a car parked under it for size. The question.
2. **Everybody's guess.** "From the soil?" A man tested it almost four
   hundred years ago. A small willow in a pot, both on scales. Five years
   of watering pass (seasons flick by). SHOW FIRST: the tree gained as
   much as a grown-up weighs. The soil lost only a small handful. "It was not the soil."
3. **Our hero arrives.** A carbon dioxide molecule drifts toward a leaf,
   the carbon in it ringed in amber. "Maybe you breathed it out this
   morning."
4. **The leaf's trick.** Zoom into the leaf. Sunlight (the app's light
   packets) streams in, water comes up from the roots. The light's energy
   splits the water; the leaf clicks carbon, hydrogen and oxygen together
   into sugar, the tree's building brick. "This is called
   photosynthesis. ... Photo. Synthesis." The oxygen left over from the
   water floats out: the air we breathe.
5. **Bricks into wood.** Sugar bricks stack into a trunk; rings appear
   one by one; our hero is locked in the wood.
6. **The answer.** The big tree again, drawn for a moment as what it is
   made of: streams of air and water flowing in, light pouring down.
   "A tree is air and water, built with sunlight."

**The picture to remember:** the two scales, with the soil's needle
almost exactly where it started.

**Info panel (Honest notes · A true story):** "made of air" means the dry wood, about half
of which is carbon, and a living tree is also about half water; a pinch
of minerals does come from the soil; the experiment is Jan Baptist van
Helmont's (1640s), who wrongly concluded "water" because nobody knew
about carbon dioxide yet; sugar becomes wood through cellulose, in many
steps.

**Changed:** "cellulose" left the narration; the weights became things a child knows (a grown-up, a
handful) with the real numbers (74 kg, 57 g) on the scales; the ending
is a picture, not a percentage.

## 💎 From pencil to diamond

- **The question:** "A pencil and a diamond are made of the very same
  atoms. So why is one soft and grey, and the other hard and sparkling?"
- **The aha:** *It is not what something is made of that decides. It is
  how its atoms hold on to each other.*
- **New words:** graphite, diamond.
- **Hero:** our carbon atom, inside the pencil's tip.

Beats:

1. **Two things on a table.** A pencil and a diamond ring. The question.
   Both glow briefly with the same label: carbon.
2. **Why a pencil writes.** Zoom into the tip: flat sheets of carbon
   atoms, like a stack of paper. Inside a sheet the atoms hold tight;
   between sheets they barely hold. Draw a line and sheets slide off onto
   the page. "This is called graphite. ... Graphite."
3. **Deep down.** The camera dives into the ground, past a depth ruler:
   houses, mines, then far below, as deep as a two-hour car ride is
   long. It is glowing hot, and the rock above squeezes from every side.
4. **A new grip.** The sheets buckle. Every atom now grabs four
   neighbours, up, down and sideways. Nothing can slide any more. "This
   is diamond. ... Diamond." (FLOW: the rearranging never freezes.)
5. **The test.** Side by side: push the graphite and it smears; push the
   diamond and nothing moves, and light bounces around inside it and
   sparkles. "The same atoms. A different way of holding hands."
6. **The way up, and the closing line.** A volcano's pipe shoots the
   diamond to the surface, fast enough for it to stay a diamond. "People
   can do this too. Machines squeeze and heat graphite until it becomes
   diamond."

**The picture to remember:** the stack of sliding sheets next to the
locked grid.

**Info panel (Honest notes · A true story):** natural diamonds form about 150 km down
and are one to three billion years old; the grid is a 3D structure drawn tilted;
the first lab diamonds were made in 1954; in 1797 Smithson Tennant burned
a diamond and got only carbon dioxide, which proved it is pure carbon.

**Changed:** the property table left the story;
depth is a car ride, not a number; the "push test" replaces the table.

## ❄️ How a snowflake is born

- **The question:** "Catch a snowflake and count its arms. Why is it
  always six?"
- **The aha:** *Because a water molecule is bent. Bent molecules can only
  fit together in rings of six, so everything they build has six sides.*
- **New words:** molecule, hexagon.
- **Hero:** one water molecule, ringed in amber.

Beats:

1. **On the mitten.** Three snowflakes land on a dark mitten, each
   different. A finger counts the arms: six, six, six. The question.
2. **The bent one.** Zoom in until one piece of water fills the screen: a
   big ball with two small ones on the same side, like a head with two
   ears. "This is a water molecule. ... Molecule. It is bent."
3. **Rings of six.** The ears are a little bit plus, the back a
   little bit minus (the ⚡ Charge playground's pull). In the cold,
   molecules slow down and hold on to each other, an ear of one against
   the back of the next. Because each is
   bent, the ring closes after exactly six. "A shape with six sides is
   called a hexagon. ... Hexagon."
4. **Growing while falling.** Zoom out: the tiny six-sided plate tumbles
   through the cloud. New molecules land on its six corners, so six arms
   grow. All six pass through the same cold and the same damp air at the
   same moment, so they grow the same way. (FLOW.)
5. **No two alike.** Two flakes fall along different paths and come out
   different. Then real photographs, one after another: all different,
   all six-armed.
6. **Closing line.** "Next time it snows, catch one on your sleeve and
   count."

**The picture to remember:** the ring closing on the sixth molecule.

**Info panel (Honest notes · A true story):** the "holding on" is called a hydrogen bond; ice is a 3D crystal and we show the view from above;
real flakes grow for tens of minutes; twelve-armed and lopsided flakes
exist, they are just rare; Wilson Bentley photographed over 5,000 flakes
from 1885; Kepler asked the six-corner question in 1611.

**Changed:** the dust seed is gone; the story now starts and ends
on the child's own mitten.

## 🏮 The neon sign

- **The question:** "This glass tube glows, but there is no fire in it
  and no light bulb. What is shining?"
- **The aha:** *The gas itself shines when electricity runs through it,
  and every gas has its own color.*
- **New words:** neon.
- **Hero:** one neon atom in the tube.

This story comes after 🎆 Firework, which already taught the electron's
jump. So it does not teach the jump again; it shows a new way to cause
it and a new thing to play with.

Beats:

1. **A dark street.** A sign that says OPEN glows red-orange. The
   question. A hand flips the switch off: the tube is clear glass, with
   "nothing" inside.
2. **Not nothing.** Zoom in: the tube is full of loose atoms drifting
   about. "This gas is called neon. ... Neon."
3. **Switch on.** Electrons (the app's blue dots) race through the tube
   and bump into the atoms. Each bumped atom does the firework's little
   dance: its electron hops up, drops back, and out comes a flash of
   red-orange light.
4. **Many make a glow.** Zoom out slowly: thousands of tiny flashes blur
   into a steady glow. (FLOW.)
5. **Neon's barcode.** The light through a prism: a handful of red and
   orange lines, neon's own fingerprint (the firework's barcode again).
6. **Change the gas.** The same tube, emptied and refilled: neon glows
   red-orange, helium peach-pink, argon pale violet. "Other gas, other
   color."
7. **Closing line.** "So a 'neon' sign that is blue or green is not
   neon at all."

**The picture to remember:** one tube, three gases, three colors.

**Info panel (Honest notes · A true story):** most blue and green signs use argon with a
little mercury and a glowing coating inside the glass; the tube holds very little gas, far thinner than air.

**Changed:** the jump close-up shrank to one beat (it is known); the
gas-swap is new and is the aha.

## 🌌 The aurora

- **The question:** "Near the North Pole, the night sky sometimes dances
  in green. Who switches it on?"
- **The aha:** *The Sun does. It throws tiny particles at us, Earth's
  magnet steers them to the poles, and where they hit the air, the air
  glows. It is a neon sign as big as the sky.*
- **New words:** aurora, solar wind.
- **Hero:** one particle from the Sun, ringed in amber.

Beats:

1. **The dancing sky.** A snowy hill, a small figure looking up, green
   curtains waving. "This is called an aurora. ... Aurora." The question.
2. **The Sun sneezes.** Far away, the Sun flings out a cloud of tiny
   particles. "This stream is called the solar wind. ... Solar. Wind." Ours gets
   its amber ring. A counter of days ticks: one,
   two. It takes about two days to get here.
3. **Earth's shield.** Earth is a giant magnet (the bar-magnet lines
   appear around it, as in the ⚡ Charge playground). The particles cannot
   come straight in. They slide along the lines, toward the top and the
   bottom of the world.
4. **Into the air.** High above the ground our particle crashes into an
   oxygen atom. The atom's electron hops up, drops back, and gives off
   green light. Thousands at once paint a curtain. (FLOW.)
5. **More colors.** Higher up, oxygen glows red; at the lower edge,
   nitrogen adds violet. Each gas of the air has its own color.
6. **Seen from space.** A glowing ring sits around the pole like a crown.
   That is why people in the far north see it, and people near the
   equator almost never do.
7. **Closing line.** "The air you breathe can glow. It only needs a push
   from the Sun."

**The picture to remember:** the particles sliding down the magnet's
lines to the pole.

**Info panel (Honest notes · A true story):** green is oxygen at about 100 km; most particles are
sped up inside Earth's magnetic field before they hit, rather than
arriving straight from the Sun; the southern lights are the same thing;
the journey takes one to three days.

**Changed:** the magnet beat is
new and explains "why near the pole", which is what children ask next.

## 💍 Where gold comes from

> **Built 2026-11-20** (roadmap step 36c): nine beats — the designed six
> plus the zoom into the ring, the teaspoon (its own show-first beat) and
> the neutron stuffing (its own beat). Closing line spoken as designed.
> The question says "no fire, no factory" — never "nobody can make gold":
> accelerators can make a few atoms (honest note).

- **The question:** "You can't make gold. Not with fire, not in any
  factory. So who made the gold in a ring?"
- **The aha:** *Two dead stars crashed into each other, long before the
  Earth existed. All the gold we have is older than our planet.*
- **New words:** neutron star.
- **Hero:** one gold atom, ringed in amber, followed backwards and then
  forwards.

Beats:

1. **A ring on a hand.** It gleams. The question. Zoom into the gold: one
   atom gets the amber ring. "Let's follow it back in time."
2. **Rewind.** The picture runs backwards, fast: the ring, a nugget in a
   river, rock, the young glowing Earth, a dust cloud, darkness. A year
   counter spins past billions.
3. **Two dead stars.** Two small dull balls circle each other. "When a
   giant star dies, it can leave this behind: a neutron star. ...
   Neutron. Star." SHOW FIRST: one teaspoon of it on a scale, a mountain
   on the other side, in balance. A city skyline beside it for size: the
   whole star is no wider than a city.
4. **The crash.** They spiral in, faster and faster, and collide. A
   blinding spray flies out. In the spray, atoms get stuffed with
   neutrons so fast that they turn into heavy new elements: platinum,
   uranium, and our hero: gold.
   (FLOW.)
5. **Forward again.** The gold drifts, gets mixed into the cloud that
   becomes the Sun and the Earth, sinks into rock, washes into a river,
   and ends on the hand.
6. **Closing line.** "The gold in a ring is older than the Earth it is
   worn on."

**The picture to remember:** the teaspoon balancing a mountain, then the
crash.

**Info panel (Honest notes · A true story):** the "stuffing" is rapid neutron capture
(the r-process); on 17 August
2017 instruments felt such a crash as a ripple in space and telescopes
then saw freshly made heavy elements in its glow; one crash probably
makes more gold than the Earth weighs; scientists still argue how much
gold comes from crashes and how much from rare kinds of exploding stars.

**Changed:** the story now runs backwards from the ring (a detective
shape that needs no setup); the periodic-table climb and the word
"kilonova" left the narration; the teaspoon and the city make "dense"
something a child can picture.

## ⚡ How a power plant works

> **Built 2026-11-20** (roadmap 35b), as designed, with one honest change:
> after the rods drop back the splits *die down* (the plant shuts itself
> down) rather than "calm down to steady": that is what a real automatic
> drop does. The fuel facts are shown as two jars of 100 uranium atoms.

- **The question:** "The bomb's chain reaction destroys a city in a
  blink. Can the very same thing light a city instead?"
- **The aha:** *Yes. Let every split cause only ONE more split, and you
  get steady heat instead of an explosion. A nuclear power plant is a
  very big kettle.*
- **New words:** control rod, turbine, generator.
- **Hero:** one neutron, ringed in amber.

This story comes after ☢️ Atomic bomb. It opens on the picture the child
already knows.

Beats:

1. **Remember this?** The bomb's chain: one split makes two, four,
   eight. The picture is a growing tree of dots that fills the screen in
   a blink. The question.
2. **The trick.** The same uranium, but with grey rods standing between
   the atoms. Follow our hero neutron: an atom splits and throws out two
   neutrons. One flies into a rod and is swallowed. The other splits one
   more atom. "These are called control rods. ... Control. Rods."
3. **One, one, one.** The dot tree again, now beside the bomb's: a single
   calm line of dots, one after another. SHOW FIRST. "Not faster, not
   slower. Just steady."
4. **A very big kettle.** Zoom out. The steady splits keep water hot. The
   water boils to steam. The steam blows on a big fan and spins it. "This
   fan is called a turbine. ... Turbine." (FLOW: the loop never stops.)
5. **Lights on.** The turbine turns a generator, and the generator makes
   electricity. It runs along wires to a little town, and the windows
   light up one by one.
6. **Safe by itself.** A hand lifts the rods too far; the line of dots
   starts to branch; the rods drop back in on their own and it calms
   down. "And this fuel can never explode like the bomb. It has far too
   little of the uranium that splits."
7. **The honest part.** Used fuel rods are lifted out, still glowing with
   danger. "The used fuel stays dangerous for a very, very long time.
   People have to guard it safely, for longer than anyone can imagine.
   That problem is not solved yet." Closing line: "Same atoms as the
   bomb. People chose to build a kettle. And they have to look after
   it."

**The picture to remember:** the wild tree of dots next to the single
calm line.

**Info panel (Honest notes · A true story):** water also slows the
neutrons, which makes them split atoms better; a few "late" neutrons are
what give the rods time to act; the automatic drop is called a scram;
one fuel pellet the size of a fingertip gives about as much energy as a
tonne of coal; the accidents at Chernobyl and Fukushima, told plainly.

**Changed:** moderation, delayed neutrons, the k meter and the word
SCRAM left the narration (they stay in the panel and in 🕹️ operator
mode, where a child can feel them); "a very big kettle" is new and is
the half of the aha the old design lacked.

## Decisions (Elena, 2026-11-19)

1. **The shared hero:** yes. Carbon looks the same across stories: the
   nucleus with symbolic electron circles, as in the bomb demos, to keep
   one visual identity.
2. **No cards** at the end of stories.
3. **No grown-ups' panel**, and no over-simplifying: the built stories
   are tested with kids and set the level. The power plant story says the
   truth about waste out loud.
4. **Gold is told backwards**, from the ring.
5. **Candle and tree share one category** (one menu item, a two-row
   chooser), like the two crystal stories.
